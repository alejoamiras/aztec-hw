Fixing what the audit found — in plain words

We red-teamed our own Ledger wallet and found 83 things to tidy before a professional auditor sees it. This is the plan to fix the serious ones, written so anyone can follow it. The full technical version is in plan.md.

The one idea behind most of it

A hardware wallet's whole job is: what you see on the little screen is exactly what you're approving. We found that the device was rock-solid at the hard maths and at refusing to sign a transaction it didn't recompute itself — but it was sloppy about showing you the full picture. A couple of things got signed that never appeared on screen, and the recipient address was shown so abbreviated that a look-alike could sneak past. Most of this plan is closing that gap, plus some spring-cleaning.

Good news the audit confirmed: the cryptography is correct (we checked every constant by hand), there are no memory bugs, and the device never signs anything it didn't independently re-derive. Those are the scary failure modes — and they're already solid. We are not touching the maths.

The work, phase by phase

P0 · Clear the clutter, fix the broken check

housekeeping

Delete a dead second wallet adapter and an old demo (code nobody uses but an auditor still has to read — and one of them even had a wrong signature). Fix the project's own automatic quality-check, which was failing and being worked around. Stop exposing internal "back doors" that let code skip the safe path.

Why first: a smaller, green codebase makes everything after it trustworthy and easy to verify.

P1 · The computer can't sneak extra things past you

no blind signing

Today, a transaction can quietly trigger extra permission-grants that get signed without a review. We make the wallet refuse to do that by default, and refuse anything carrying hidden extras. We also write the tests that prove the most important safety guard actually works.

Why: "no blind signing" — the thing you most care about. Nothing gets your approval unless it was shown to you.

P2 · The device backstop + tidy hardening

device · firmware

Add a "Blind signing" switch in the device Settings, OFF by default (exactly like Ledger's Ethereum app). With it off, the device refuses to sign anything it can't show you; turning it on requires going into Settings yourself and shows a big warning every time. Plus: tighten a couple of checks, and add a limit on how often the secret-key-derivation can be hammered (slows down a physical attacker).

Why a switch and not just "always refuse": it gives you the choice for advanced cases later, the honest Ledger way — without ever signing blind behind your back.

P3 · Show the whole truth on screen

device · screen

Show the recipient address as 8+8 bytes (was 4+4 — too easy to fake) with a "show full address" tap for the cautious. Show the real amount next to the pretty one so a bad "decimals" value can't make a big transfer look tiny. Add the missing screen for the faucet action. Make the "verified" label honest about what was verified. Fix the "reveal viewing key" wording so it tells the truth about what it shares.

Why: this is the screen you actually read. We give the risky bits more room without burying you in detail.

P4 · The one careful surgery (kept separate on purpose)

device · the risky bit

The device's "this transaction is from your account" check currently assumes a default account setup. We make it work for any account by sending the account's salt and type to the device — but the device re-derives the account itself and refuses if it doesn't match. It never just trusts what the computer claims.

Why isolated + last: it changes the messages between computer and device, so it's the highest-risk change — it gets its own revertible step, lands on top of everything already proven, and the device must be updated first.

P5 · Privacy & supply-chain polish

cleanup

Quieter logging (stop printing your activity to the browser console), be honest about which server your transactions go through, register a real ID instead of a placeholder, and pin our build tools so results stay reproducible.

Why: small leaks and loose ends an auditor will flag — cheap to close now.

P6 · Prove it all still works

validation

Re-run the whole flow (deploy / faucet / transfer, both signing schemes, switch on and off) on the emulator and live test network, re-run the fuzzing, and have a fresh codex review the finished work.

Why: we changed the device firmware — nothing ships until the full matrix is green again.

Three decisions worth a second look

These came up while three independent planners (a human + two AIs) compared notes. You should weigh in:

1. The "Blind signing" switch — keep it, or remove that path entirely?
You chose the switch (Ledger-standard, future-proof). One planner argued that since nothing today needs blind signing, even the off-by-default switch is needless surface, and we should just delete the raw-sign feature for now. We'll build the switch as you chose — but if you'd rather be maximally minimal, say so.
2. Send the account "type" to the device too, not just the salt?
You said carry the salt. A planner pointed out that salt-alone still hard-codes today's account type — so the fix would be only half-done. We're planning to carry both (the complete fix). It's a slightly bigger change to the device messages; confirm that's fine.
3. Make transfers "cancellable" to stop a fee-replay?
There's a small risk a transaction could be replayed to spend the fee-sponsor's money again. Marking transfers "cancellable" closes it for free (no screen change). It slightly changes how a transfer behaves under the hood; confirm you're OK with it.

What we deliberately are NOT doing

We're not faking a "narrower" viewing-key reveal — the Aztec protocol derives all the privacy keys from one secret, so a partial reveal isn't really possible; we tell the truth in the wording instead. We're not touching the (correct) cryptography maths. And we're not papering over the one thing only Ledger can fix: the deepest side-channel certification, which stays documented for the auditor.
