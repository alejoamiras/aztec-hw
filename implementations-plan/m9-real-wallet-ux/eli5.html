Milestone 9 · Real-wallet UX

Make the Ledger feel like a real Aztec wallet

Multiple accounts, and a device that talks in addresses — not in cryptic paths.

The one-sentence goal

M8 proved the Ledger can be your whole Aztec wallet. M9 makes it pleasant: hold more than one account on the same device, and make every screen the Ledger shows speak the language you actually recognize — your account address.

1. More than one account

Like the "Account 1 / Account 2 / Account 3" you already see in other Ledger apps.

Your Ledger can grow a whole family of accounts from its one seed, just by changing a small number (the "account index"): index 0, index 1, index 2… Each index gives a completely separate Aztec account, and — because it's deterministic — the same index always re-grows the same account. So "Account #1" is always your Account #1, on any computer, forever.

Why an index, not a saved file. We could have let each account use a random "salt" and saved it somewhere — but then losing that file loses the account. An index is a tiny number that's part of the standard derivation, re-grown from the device every time. Nothing to save, nothing to lose — same principle as M8.

The app will also notice when an account is already set up on the network and skip the "Deploy" step — so you're not offered to create an account that already exists (which is exactly what blocked deploying a fresh one for the demo).

2. The device speaks addresses, not paths

Today some device screens show the technical derivation path (m/44'/…). Nobody thinks in paths. People think: "I'm signing as this account." So every review screen will lead with Account #N and your account address (shown short, like 0x0aa630…3dbe), and the path drops out of the way.

The honest, careful bit. The little ✓ matters: the device doesn't just print the address the computer claims — it re-computes the address itself and refuses to continue if the computer lied. (It already does this when creating an account; M9 brings the same guarantee to sending.) A wallet that shows you an address it didn't verify is a wallet that can be tricked into showing you a safe-looking lie.

3. Being honest about where your "viewing key" lives

A truthfulness fix. Our second-opinion review (Codex) caught us slightly over-claiming. The key that lets the browser read your private balance has to live in the browser's memory while you use it — there's no way around that (the browser needs it to decrypt your notes). So we'll stop implying it's locked away or "never touches the computer." What stays true and important: your spending key never leaves the Ledger, so the worst a hacked browser can do is peek, never spend. "Forget session" clears what it can and the wording will say exactly that — no more, no less.

4. A demo page that tells the story

No flashy redesign — we keep the current clean look and spend the effort where it counts: an account picker, clear "is this deployed yet?" states, and a recovery moment that's obvious on camera — wipe everything → reconnect the Ledger → your account and balance come back. The point of the page is to make the sovereignty story legible to someone watching for the first time.

How it's built (the order)

  • Accounts first: the index picker, "already deployed?" detection, and a fix so each account's keys are cached separately (a different account/device always asks the Ledger fresh).
  • Easy screens next: drop the path from the create-account and reveal screens (they already show the address).
  • The careful screen: teach the device to verify + show the sending account's address — the one real piece of device work.
  • Then the page polish + the honesty wording.

What we're double-checking

  • That adding a field to the "sign these calls" message doesn't quietly break the existing send/deploy flows.
  • That "is this account deployed?" is a reliable signal (and doesn't hide a needed Deploy).
  • That we're not now under-claiming — i.e. there isn't a real way to protect the viewing key better that we're waving away.
